THE DESIGN QUESTION

A worker with a broken pedal was still moving goods. The question became: how do we improve the vehicle already earning a living?

A new electric vehicle may be a good product and still be out of reach for someone who works on daily cash flow, cannot afford a long break from work, and relies on familiar local repairs. Tirare starts from that existing asset rather than from a new chassis.

WHAT THE RETROFIT IS DESIGNED TO PRESERVE

  • The existing rear axle and wheel alignment
  • Pedals, chain, steering and brakes
  • Manual rideability if electric assist is unavailable
  • Local installation and repair as design requirements

THE HUMAN POWER PATH

01Pedals + crankRider force becomes torque.
02ChainTransfers torque to the rear drive.
03Fixed sprocketOn the reference trike, coupled directly to the axle.
04Solid rear axleDrives both rear wheels; no differential in this layout.

If the motor turns the axle, a sprocket rigidly fixed to it can turn the chain and pedals too. MRII's drivetrain architecture accounts for this back-driving constraint while keeping the rider-input measurement distinct from powered-side torque.

Component roles and protected interfaces

Crank and pedals provide rider input. The chain carries tensile load. The sprocket converts chain force into shaft torque. The rear axle transmits torque to the wheels and also sits within a structural system of bearings, wheel mounts, brakes and frame reactions. The frame and cargo bed carry the load; the steering and brakes remain essential vehicle systems, not optional packaging space.

These tricycles vary by maker and workshop. Confirm the exact sprocket attachment, axle diameter, bearing arrangement, brake hardware and frame geometry on each reference vehicle before finalising an interface.

03 / THE MRII MECHANICAL IDEA

The rider signal must
survive powered drive.

MRII measures rider input mechanically while the drivetrain continues carrying torque through the vehicle.

A KNOWN ONE-WAY DRIVE PRINCIPLE

A freewheel lets the rider drive without forcing the pedals to follow the powered axle.

In a common bicycle freewheel, spring-loaded pawls engage ratchet teeth during pedaling. When the wheel outruns the sprocket, the pawls overrun and the pedals can remain still. This is an established mechanism principle, not the invention being presented here.

In the reference cargo tricycle, the sprocket is fixed directly to the solid axle. If the axle is driven by a motor, the chain and pedals can be back-driven. MRII's torque path has to account for that constraint.

PEDAL LEADSEngage

Human torque should pass from sprocket to axle.

AXLE LEADSOverrun

Motor-driven axle should not force the pedals to rotate.

Ratchet-and-pawl vs. sprag/roller clutch

Ratchet and pawl: familiar, widely used and often economical. Torque is carried through discrete tooth engagement; tooth geometry, pawl springs, engagement angle, wear and impact loading matter.

Sprag or roller-ramp clutch: one-way action is produced by wedge engagement between controlled inner and outer races. It can be compact and engage without ratchet-tooth stepping, but torque capacity depends on raceway geometry, hardness, finish, tolerances, lubrication and alignment. A raw, unprepared axle is not automatically a suitable clutch race.

Role within MRII: an overrunning torque-transmission element is placed downstream of the compliant rider-input interface; the powered actuator enters farther downstream. The familiar one-way mechanism is a functional element in the architecture. MRII is the rider-intent measurement and torque-isolation system.

04 / TIRARE'S ARCHITECTURE

Three connected jobs.
One retrofit.

Tirare's system has to separate rider input, one-way pedal drive and electric assistance without replacing the tricycle's core structure.

01

MECHANICAL TORQUE PATH

Keep the drivetrain legible.

The existing pedals and chain continue transmitting human torque. A known overrunning element forms part of the MRII torque path so powered-side drive can be separated from the rider-input interface.

Architecture defined; integration testing remains
02

MRII · RIDER-INTENT MEASUREMENT

Measure effort mechanically.

A compliant coupling twists under rider torque; an AS5047P measures relative angle. After calibration, that angular signal can estimate rider input.

Physical subsystem demonstrated
03

ELECTRIC ASSIST

Add torque, not a speed command.

A frame-mounted motor adds assist downstream. Pedals, chain, steering and brakes remain; the planned control is rider-requested torque assistance, not speed control.

Integrated vehicle still to validate

MECHANICAL RIDER INTENT INTERFACE

MRII is the sensing architecture—not the entire vehicle.

The demonstrated subsystem uses a compliant flexure coupling and an AS5047P magnetic angle sensor to measure relative rotation. A downstream overrunning element is intended to limit powered-side torque influencing the rider-input signal.

The engineering question is whether that signal remains useful and isolated under matched load conditions after the mechanism is integrated into the drivetrain.

THE IMPORTANT DISTINCTION

MRII is not a replacement powertrain. Human torque continues mechanically through the input member and compliant coupling toward the drivetrain; the small relative twist supplies the measurement signal. The downstream overrunning element is intended to keep motor-side torque from corrupting that rider-input signal.

See the baseline force path ↑
MRII measurement path

Human torque → input member → compliant torsional coupling → output member → overrunning torque-transmission element → downstream drivetrain. The powered actuator enters downstream of the overrunning mechanism.

Angular displacement is the measured quantity; physical torque depends on calibration and the stiffness of the assembled coupling. Numerical analysis is not a substitute for traceable calibration and synchronized physical testing.

05 / MOTOR AND REDUCTION

Use proven parts.
Match them to the load.

Current direction: a conventional DC motor with a reduction gearbox, prioritising attainable cost, practical sourcing and serviceability.

01

DC motor + reduction gearbox

Following technical feedback from Professor Andy Ruina, Tirare is prioritising a conventional DC motor paired with a reduction gearbox. The goal is to use practical components and match the motor's operating speed to the high torque and low speed required at the cargo tricycle's axle.

02

Why the gearbox matters

The motor can run at a more practical speed while the reduction stage increases output torque and reduces output speed. Gear ratio must be selected against loaded starting torque, intended climbing ability, wheel speed, payload and the motor's continuous thermal limits—not chosen from nominal watts alone.

03

Mounting is part of the drivetrain

The motor and gearbox need a stable mounting arrangement, a defined torque-reaction path into suitable frame members, and a serviceable coupling to the existing drivetrain. The mount must avoid compromising axle bearings, brakes, chain alignment or normal manual operation.

Initial planning envelope

The early planning range is 250–350 W continuous, at least 500 W peak, and a 36–48 V LFP battery around 400–600 Wh. These are preliminary targets, not a frozen specification. Final motor, controller, gear ratio and battery selection must follow measurements of loaded wheel torque, route gradients, payload, duty cycle, heat rejection and energy used per route.

What the next sizing pass must calculate

Required tractive force at the tyre; wheel torque at breakaway and on the design gradient; wheel speed across the intended working range; motor speed and torque after reduction; gearbox efficiency and peak torque rating; thermal performance over a working shift; controller current; battery usable energy; and the bracket and coupling loads. Compare supplier quotes for the complete motor, gearbox, controller and mounting assembly—not the motor price alone.

What the payment model must cover

Hardware and assembly; installation; quality checks; battery purchase and replacement reserve; charging and swap operations; workshop margin; maintenance and warranty; collection costs; payment defaults; financing; and service downtime. The tariff should be tested against the operator's net work income, not merely against the price of a new EV.

The vehicle must remain manually usable when electric assistance is unavailable; payment collection should not make an otherwise operable vehicle unusable.

07 / FROM DESIGN TO PILOT

Prove the mechanism.
Then measure the work.

The pilot should answer technical and livelihood questions with recorded evidence, not assumptions.

GATE 01

Bench tests

Verify drive/overrun direction, torque capacity, overrun drag, wear, heat, dust tolerance and failure behavior. Calibrate MRII with a traceable torque reference.

GATE 02

Vehicle integration

Check packaging, frame/mount loads, axle and bearings, brakes, steering, wiring, hard disconnect, manual operation with assist off, and installation repeatability.

GATE 03

Controlled operator trial

After engineering review and the correct regulatory pathway: consented use, defined stop criteria, trained service support, baseline measurements and follow-up logs.

WHAT THE PILOT MEASURES

Does the system help
without shifting the risk?

Torque signal & isolationUptime, failures & serviceEnergy / swap frequencyTrips, payload & distanceFatigue & perceived effortNet income & operating costInstall time & workshop fitWillingness to pay / continued use
Field-study method: the minimum credible comparison

Record a baseline for each participating vehicle before retrofit: ownership, route, load/payload, shift duration, trips, distance, income, costs, breakdowns and self-reported effort/fatigue. Repeat at defined intervals after installation, preserving comparable route and workload context where feasible. Log battery swaps, service events, component changes and incidents. Track retention and willingness to pay, not just initial acceptance.

Use paired before/after records and report missing data and confounders. Without a suitable comparison design, describe results as observed changes rather than causal impact. The DIV application proposes a pilot-scale evaluation; the actual number of vehicles should follow prototype readiness, funding, partner capacity and the safety gate.
